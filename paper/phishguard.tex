\begin{abstract}
Security operations centers drown in suspicious email. Every false positive
costs an analyst minutes they do not have. And a bare verdict such as
``malicious: 92\%'' is useless in an incident report. There is nothing to
quote and nothing to defend. PhishGuard is an open-source triage platform
built around that reality. Its detection core pairs a deterministic 29-rule
heuristic engine, aimed at phishing indicators, with TF-IDF statistical
classifiers in a fixed weighted ensemble, and every decision ships with an
analyst-readable report listing the evidence behind it.

We benchmarked the core on five public corpora, 108,116 emails in total,
spanning spam, phishing, and advance-fee fraud. The collection is curated for
phishing-email research \cite{phishcurated,champa2024curated}, but the label
semantics differ per corpus: the positive class means \textit{spam} for
TREC\_07, CEAS\_08, and SpamAssassin, \textit{phishing} for Nazario\_5, and
\textit{advance-fee fraud} for Nigerian\_5. We report scores against each
corpus's own positive class. No pooled ``phishing detection'' number is
claimed. Evaluation uses stratified 5-fold cross-validation with identical
splits for every model. Baselines: a PILFER-style heuristic feature set with
random forest, TF-IDF with logistic regression, and TF-IDF with a linear SVM.

The hybrid reaches F1 scores of 0.908--0.986 on the per-corpus positive class
and takes the best precision on four of five corpora (0.985--1.000). The
TF-IDF SVM wins F1 on all five (0.984--0.998). That gap is the finding, not
an embarrassment. The rule layer trades 1 to 14 points of recall for
precision and per-indicator evidence. On these corpora and splits, where
false alarms are expensive, that is a trade worth making. We do not claim it
proves superiority in operational SOC environments. Code, protocol, and
results are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Phishing remains the most common first foothold in enterprise breaches. The
detection side of the problem is well studied. The triage side is not. Once
something looks suspicious, an analyst gets minutes to decide: escalate,
contain, or dismiss. Two things decide whether a tool survives that moment.
Precision comes first. A false positive is never free. It burns analyst time,
breeds alert fatigue, and slowly teaches the team to ignore the tooling.
Evidence comes second. A score with no reasoning cannot go into an incident
report. The analyst needs to see why, in words they can quote.

The literature attacked detection from both ends. Fette \textit{et al.}'s
PILFER combined ten hand-crafted deception features with machine learning
\cite{pilfer}. Zhang \textit{et al.}'s CANTINA derived TF-IDF lexical
signatures from page content and checked them against a search engine
\cite{cantina}. URL-lexical ML pipelines have held up as a durable baseline
ever since \cite{sahingoz2019}. Then explainability became a goal in its own
right. EXPLICATE couples a classifier with LIME/SHAP explanations rendered
into plain language by a large language model \cite{explicate}. What we could
not find was an open, deployable triage system aimed at the analyst instead
of the end user, evaluated on public corpora spanning spam, phishing, and
fraud, that reports the full cost of bolting a deterministic rule layer onto
statistical classifiers rather than claiming a win on accuracy.

PhishGuard is that system. A FastAPI/React/MongoDB platform whose detection
core fuses a 29-rule phishing-indicator engine with TF-IDF classifiers under
a fixed weighted vote, with evidence-bearing analyst reports on every
verdict. Three contributions. First, the system itself: deployable,
precision-oriented email triage combining deterministic phishing indicators
with statistical classifiers. Second, a public benchmark of the detection
core, evaluated separately on public spam, phishing, and advance-fee-fraud
corpora (108,116 emails), against three baselines, all on identical
cross-validation splits, with per-corpus label semantics stated explicitly.
Third, an honest account of where the hybrid helps (precision, evidence) and
where it hurts (recall, F1) next to pure statistical models. Everything
needed to reproduce the work is released.

Section~II covers related work, Section~III the architecture, Section~IV the
evaluation protocol, Section~V the results, Section~VI limitations and future
work, and Section~VII concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Heuristics and statistical detection.} PILFER \cite{pilfer}
introduced ten features aimed at deceptive technique: IP-based URLs, domain
age, mismatched link targets, link and domain counts. With SVM and
random-forest classifiers it reported over 96\% phishing detection at roughly
0.1\% false positives against the filters of its day. CANTINA \cite{cantina}
took the content route instead, deriving TF-IDF lexical signatures from page
text and verifying them through a search engine. Sahingoz \textit{et al.}
\cite{sahingoz2019} later showed that URL-lexical features with classical
classifiers remain competitive. Our PILFER-style baseline reimplements that
heuristic spirit with ten URL/HTML features. Domain-age and WHOIS lookups are
left out; the evaluation ran with no network access.

\textbf{Explainable phishing detection.} EXPLICATE \cite{explicate} pairs a
classifier built on domain-specific features with a dual LIME/SHAP
explanation layer, translated by DeepSeek v3 into accessible language. It
reports 98.4\% accuracy alongside measured explanation quality, shipped as a
GUI and browser extension for end users. The audience is the difference.
PhishGuard writes for the SOC analyst: indicators with quoted matched text,
URL and sender findings, a recommended action. The explanation lives inside
an alert-triage workflow, not a consumer tool. We describe these reports as a
system feature; without a user study we make no claim that they measurably
improve analyst decisions or save time.

\textbf{Evaluation practice.} Too many detectors are evaluated on private or
single corpora. Comparison becomes impossible. The TREC Spam Track was an
early attempt at a shared framework with public corpora such as trec07p
\cite{trec07}. We evaluate on the public \textit{Phishing Email Curated
Datasets} collection (Zenodo DOI 10.5281/zenodo.8339691)
\cite{phishcurated,champa2024curated}, which spans the TREC 2007 and CEAS
2008 tracks plus Nazario, Nigerian-fraud, and SpamAssassin messages. The
collection is presented for phishing-email research, but its constituent
labels are not all phishing labels; Section~IV states the per-corpus
semantics explicitly, and the exact protocol and code are published with this
paper.

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline. Three design goals shaped it. No
black boxes: whatever the system claims, the analyst must see why.
Deterministic signals fire fast and never depend on model state. And the
whole thing runs self-hosted, triaging each email in under a second.

\subsection{Rule Engine}
29 compiled regular-expression rules in ten categories (Table~\ref{tab:rules}),
aimed at phishing indicators: credential harvesting, brand impersonation,
urgency and threat language, financial-fraud lures, and technical deception.
Each match carries a severity, low to critical, and a confidence. A 0--100
risk score and 3-class probabilities (safe/suspicious/phishing) come from
severity-weighted aggregation. Typical latency is about 5\,ms per email.

\subsection{ML Classifier}
TF-IDF over the email text, 10,000 features, 1--2 grams, sublinear TF. On top
of it sit logistic regression (C=1.0, class-weighted) and a random forest
(100 trees, max depth 12); their probabilities are averaged. Per-word
coefficients are exposed so the dashboard can show feature importance.
Typical latency is about 50\,ms.

\subsection{Ensemble}
Without the optional transformer, the final score is a fixed weighted vote:
\begin{equation}
P = 0.35 \cdot P_{\text{rules}} + 0.65 \cdot P_{\text{ML}},
\label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}
with $P_{\text{ML}}$ the averaged LR/RF probability. A DistilBERT layer
exists at weight 0.40, rebalancing the mix to 0.25/0.35/0.40, but it was not
trained for this build. It is switched off and excluded from every number
reported here. The weights are shipped defaults. They were not tuned on the
benchmark corpora.

\subsection{Explanation and Platform}
URL analysis scores every link for suspiciousness. Sender-trust scoring and
NLP urgency features complete the picture. An LLM explainer turns all of it
into an analyst-style report: executive summary, classification with
confidence, indicators with quoted evidence, URL and sender findings, attack
type, recommended actions. These reports are a system feature; we do not
claim measured improvements in analyst speed or decision quality. Around the
core sits the platform. 17 REST endpoints. A React dashboard with threat
overview, split-pane scanner, and searchable history exporting to CSV and
JSON. MongoDB persistence. A feedback endpoint where analysts can correct
verdicts.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Corpora and label semantics}
Table~\ref{tab:corpora} summarizes the five corpora from
\cite{phishcurated,champa2024curated} (1 = positive class, 0 = negative class).
The collection is curated for phishing-email research, but the positive class
does \textit{not} mean phishing everywhere. For TREC\_07, CEAS\_08, and
SpamAssassin it means \textit{spam}, inherited from their source spam
evaluations (TREC 2007 Spam Track public corpus trec07p \cite{trec07}, the
CEAS 2008 challenge, and the SpamAssassin public corpus). For Nazario\_5 it
means \textit{phishing}, and for Nigerian\_5 \textit{advance-fee fraud}. We
verified the semantics against the source tracks and by inspecting labeled
examples (e.g., TREC\_07 positives are pharmaceutical spam; Nazario\_5
positives are credential-harvesting lures). Every file was MD5-checked
against the Zenodo-published checksums (v1). Email text is subject plus body.
HTML is kept as-is so the link-manipulation features see the original
markup.

\subsection{Protocol}
Stratified 5-fold cross-validation, random state 42, with the \textbf{same
splits for every model}. The TF-IDF vectorizer is fit on each training fold
only. No test vocabulary leaks into training. We report precision,
recall, and F1 on the positive class in Table~III, all on pooled
out-of-fold predictions; the released benchmark outputs additionally record
accuracy and macro F1 for every model. The corpora are binary, so the system's
\textit{suspicious} class is not exercised here. On leakage: the heuristic
rule engine is a deterministic, non-fitted component built from predefined
regular-expression rules with hardcoded severity weights. Its scores are
computed independently for each email and use no labels, corpus-level
statistics, or information from the evaluation folds. TF-IDF vectorization
and classifier fitting use only the training partition of each fold, and the
resulting transformations are applied to that fold's held-out partition.
Exact duplicates are rare
(0.14\% of TREC\_07 at most, none with conflicting labels); a
deduplicated-before-split sensitivity rerun is reported in Section~V.

\subsection{Baselines}
\textit{PILFER-style:} ten hand-crafted URL/HTML features. IP-literal URL,
URL and domain counts, mismatched anchor/href, `@' in URL, subdomain depth,
HTML forms, JavaScript, longest URL, HTTPS ratio. Balanced random forest. It
reimplements the \cite{pilfer} idea, not the verbatim feature set.
\textit{TF-IDF+LR:} the system's own ML configuration, retrained per fold.
\textit{TF-IDF+SVM:} the same TF-IDF features with a balanced linear SVM.
\textit{PhishGuard hybrid:} the production rule engine, imported verbatim
from the codebase, combined per \eqref{eq:ensemble} and thresholded at 0.5.

The repository also ships a 130-email demonstration set, 50 safe, 35
suspicious, 45 phishing, for pipeline validation. Its cross-validation
figures (LR F1 $0.83 \pm 0.06$, RF F1 $0.85 \pm 0.09$) describe the demo.
They are \textbf{not} benchmark accuracy and are not presented as results.

The two large spam corpora tell a consistent story. On TREC\_07 and CEAS\_08
every model is strong. The hybrid takes the best precision observed anywhere,
0.996 and 0.999, with F1 of 0.986 and 0.980. Just behind the TF-IDF SVM on
both. On the phishing corpus, Nazario\_5, the cost appears: hybrid recall
drops to 0.877 while precision holds at 0.998. The rule layer's conservatism
vetoes true positives its statistical half would have caught. SpamAssassin is
the hardest case for the hybrid: recall 0.842, F1 0.908. That makes sense. It
is general spam, not phishing, and the rule engine is tuned for phishing
indicators, so its contribution shrinks. The PILFER-style baseline behaves
the way classical heuristics do. High recall, 0.836 to 0.964, with clearly
lower precision, 0.624 to 0.949.

No claim of accuracy superiority here. The hybrid does not win on F1. What
the numbers show is a documented trade, measured separately on spam,
phishing, and fraud corpora. The deterministic rule layer buys the highest
precision and per-indicator evidence at a recall cost of 1 to 14 points
depending on the corpus. On these corpora and splits, for triage work where
false positives are expensive analyst time, that is a defensible place to
stand. It is not proof of superiority in an operational SOC.

\subsection{Duplicate sensitivity}
Exact duplicates (normalized subject plus body) are rare: 74 of 53,757
messages in TREC\_07 at most, none with conflicting labels. To check whether
duplicates crossing train/test folds inflate the scores, we reran the full
protocol with exact duplicates removed \textit{before} the stratified split
(53,683 kept for TREC\_07; 39,145 for CEAS\_08; 3,063 for Nazario\_5; 6,315
for Nigerian\_5; 5,809 for SpamAssassin), keeping every other setting
identical. The largest per-model F1 movement was 0.0029 (Nazario\_5 hybrid,
0.9333 to 0.9362); all other deltas were at most 0.0012, and SpamAssassin was
bit-identical. The best-precision and best-F1 orderings were unchanged on all
five corpora. Duplicates do not drive the findings.

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work}
The corpora are binary. So the system's three-way output,
safe/suspicious/phishing, could not be fully evaluated. How the
\textit{suspicious} band behaves in practice is unmeasured. A graded-alert
corpus would be a worthwhile follow-up. The DistilBERT layer sat out this
build, untrained. Running it through the same protocol is the obvious next
experiment. The rule-engine thresholds and the 0.35/0.65 ensemble weights are
shipped defaults, never tuned. Per-deployment calibration should recover some
of the recall gap. Cross-dataset evaluation, training on one corpus and
testing on another, would test generalization harder than random folds do;
it is future work. Triage-time reduction in a real SOC is unmeasured. A
protocol for the author's SOC lab exists: 30 real Wazuh alerts, baseline
versus assisted triage, timed, with a washout period. The study has not run,
so no such claim is made. Finally, the corpora are English and partly dated.
Modern spear-phishing, non-English lures, and adversarial evasion of regex
rules are all untested.

\section{Conclusion}
PhishGuard is an open, deployable malicious-email triage platform. Its hybrid
core, evaluated separately on public spam, phishing, and advance-fee-fraud
corpora (108,116 emails) against three baselines, leads on precision at
0.985 to 1.000 while trailing pure TF-IDF models on F1. An honest, measured
trade-off on these corpora and splits. The system ships with
evidence-bearing analyst reports as a built-in feature. Code, protocol, and
results are released for reproduction.
